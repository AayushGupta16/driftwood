Outsourced SDR

Outsourced SDR means paying an outside firm to do your sales development: building lead lists, sending cold outreach in your name, and booking meetings for your team. This page covers what the services include, what they commonly cost, what can go wrong, and the two alternatives: hiring in-house, or buying the same outcome as software.

What is an outsourced SDR service?

SDR stands for sales development representative: the person at the top of a sales team who finds prospects and starts conversations that someone else closes. Outsourcing that job means hiring a firm, usually called an outsourced SDR company or an SDR-as-a-service provider, to run it for you. What you are buying is speed and coverage without headcount: the firm already has trained reps, sending infrastructure, and data tools, so a program can start in weeks.

A typical engagement covers four things.

  1. List building: identifying the accounts and contacts that fit your target profile, usually from the firm's own data tools.
  2. Outreach: writing and sending email and LinkedIn sequences on your behalf, and on many plans, cold calling.
  3. Qualification: handling replies, screening for fit, and getting interested prospects to agree to a meeting.
  4. Handoff: booked meetings land on your team's calendar, and your closers take over.

Engagements usually run on a monthly retainer with a quarterly or longer commitment, and the deliverable most firms anchor on is meetings booked.

What does an outsourced SDR service cost?

Most firms quote after a call rather than publishing pricing, so any number here is approximate. As of mid-2026, commonly cited retainers run about $3,000 to $10,000 a month. The low end usually means a shared or offshore rep; the high end usually means a dedicated senior rep with a manager over them. Some firms price per meeting booked instead, and some layer a per-meeting fee on top of a base retainer.

One comparison worth making before you sign: at the top of that band, a year of retainers costs about as much as a year of an in-house SDR, without building anything you keep when the contract ends.

What to watch for with outsourced SDR companies

These are structural to the agency model, not bad behavior by any one firm. Good firms will discuss all four plainly, and the questions are worth asking before you commit to a quarter.

Shared reps across accounts

Dedicated is a spectrum. At many firms your rep splits time across several client accounts, and the person who sold you the program is not the person who will send your outreach. Ask directly how many accounts your rep carries and who actually writes the messages.

Generic scripts

The firm's playbook is its product, and it gets reused across clients. Your outreach can end up reading like every other outbound message in your prospect's inbox, because it came from the same template library. Ask to see real messages sent for other clients, not the sample sequence in the deck.

Your domain's reputation in someone else's hands

If the firm sends from your domain, their volume and any spam complaints attach to your name, and repairing a burned domain takes months. If they send from domains and accounts they own, the replies, relationships, and history accumulate somewhere you do not control, and they leave when the contract ends. Either way, ask who owns the accounts, the lead list, and the reply history on the way out.

Activity metrics instead of outcomes

Dashboards tend to count sends and dials, because those are easy to guarantee. The number that matters is qualified meetings with people you actually want to sell to. Put that number in the contract, not the activity.

Hiring an SDR in-house instead

The classic alternative. A US-based SDR typically costs $80,000 to $120,000 a year once salary, commission, tools, and management are counted. Ramp to full productivity is commonly cited at around three months, and average SDR tenure is short, often under two years, so recruiting recurs. Someone senior also has to manage and coach them; an SDR without a manager and a working playbook mostly generates activity.

What you get for all that is compounding: the rep learns your product and market, the learning stays inside the company, and judgment improves over time in a way you own. The hire works best when you already know your messaging works and need more of it. It works worst as the experiment itself, because the experiment costs six figures a year plus management time before you find out.

Who should still pick an agency

An agency remains the right call in some situations. If your motion depends on cold calling, hire the firm with trained callers; driftwood works LinkedIn and email, not phones. If you want outbound entirely off your plate, an agency gets closer: driftwood keeps a person on your side approving sends, which takes minutes a day but is not zero. And if what you sell is a service or a relationship rather than a product someone can see working, a demo-led approach has less to show, and an experienced human rep may earn the meeting better. If none of those describe you, the software option covers the same ground for less money, with your accounts and your reputation staying yours.

Is outsourcing SDR worth it?

It depends on what an outside rep can credibly say on your behalf. Outsourcing works best when the message is simple, the market is broad, and you need volume quickly. It disappoints most often when the pitch needs product depth the outside rep does not have, which shows up as plenty of activity and few qualified replies. Whichever way you go, judge the program on qualified meetings, not sends or dials.

Outsourced SDR vs in-house: which should you pick?

An agency starts in weeks and skips recruiting and management, but the learning stays with the firm. An in-house SDR typically costs $80,000 to $120,000 a year fully loaded and takes months to ramp, but keeps product knowledge and judgment inside your company. A common path is to outsource while proving the market, then hire once the playbook works. The risk in that plan is that a generic outside playbook never proves anything either way.
