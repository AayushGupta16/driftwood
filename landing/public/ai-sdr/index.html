What is an AI SDR?

An AI SDR is software that does the outbound work of a sales development representative: researching prospects, writing outreach, sending it, and following up on replies. The tools split into two kinds: autonomous AI SDRs, which send on their own, and assisted AI SDRs, where a human approves every send before it goes out.

What does an AI SDR actually do?

SDR stands for sales development representative: the person at the top of a sales team whose job is to find prospects and start conversations, which someone else then closes. An AI SDR is software doing that job. The job breaks into four parts, and most tools cover some or all of them.

  1. Research: reading what is public about the person and the company, and deciding whether and why to reach out.
  2. Drafting: writing the message for that specific prospect, usually for email or LinkedIn.
  3. Sending: scheduling sends, managing deliverability, and rotating inboxes where the tool uses its own accounts.
  4. Follow-up: bumping non-responders, spotting replies, and handing real conversations to a person.

Tools differ less on this list than on two other questions: what the message actually carries, and whether a human approves each send before it goes out. The second question splits the category in two; it gets its own section below.

AI SDR, AI sales agent, AI BDR: one category, several names

AI sales agent and AI sales rep describe the same tools. Agent usually signals software that plans and acts in a loop rather than running a fixed sequence, and some vendors stretch the label to cover inbound and full-cycle selling, but for cold outbound the products are the same category. AI BDR meant the same thing too and is fading from use: in Google's Keyword Planner data pulled July 2026, US searches for "ai bdr" are down about 90% year over year while "ai sdr" holds steady. Whatever the label on the website, you are evaluating the same kind of software, and the same four questions later on this page apply.

What AI SDRs do well and badly today

The honest version of both halves.

What they do well: coverage and consistency. An agent can research more prospects in an evening than a rep covers in a month, it never forgets a follow-up, it works at whatever hour the prospect's timezone wakes up, and its cost per message rounds to zero. The research itself has gotten genuinely good: reading a company's site, docs, job posts, and news and producing an accurate picture is something current models do reliably.

What they do badly: the message. Most tools in the category compete on volume, and their personalization is copy-deep: a merge tag fills in the first name, a scraped detail supplies the opener, a language model restates your homepage in a friendly tone. It costs nothing to produce and it reads that way, and prospects now pattern-match the style and delete it unread. The numbers bear this out. We collect the public studies, with a named source and year for every figure, on our cold outbound benchmarks page: at average quality and at scale, cold email replies now measure under 1% of sends, 0.45% across 7.5 million emails in one 2026 study, while small, heavily personalized campaigns still measure between 5% and 18%. The spread between those two numbers is the whole game, and volume tools sit on the wrong side of it.

There are also costs the dashboard does not show: burned sending domains, mail that quietly lands in spam, and lead lists that stop being usable because everyone on them has already deleted three of your sequences. More sends is not the same thing as more replies.

Autonomous vs assisted AI SDRs

The most useful split in the category is who approves the send. Vendor pages rarely put it in those words, so here are the two kinds plainly.

An autonomous AI SDR sources prospects, writes the messages, and sends them on its own. You set the targeting and the playbook up front and review results after the fact. The bet is volume: more prospects touched per dollar than any human team could match. 11x's Alice, Artisan's Ava, and AiSDR work this way.

An assisted AI SDR does the same work, the research, the drafting, the follow-up, but a human approves every send before it goes out. The bet is quality: nothing reaches a prospect unread, so the agent can take bigger swings with each message, and a bad one gets caught in review instead of in a prospect's inbox. driftwood (driftwood.sh) is an assisted AI SDR, so read this page knowing which side we sit on.

Which kind fits depends on what a mistake costs you. A fifty-person sales org running a proven playbook can absorb a clumsy message; the volume pays for it. A founder sending from their own LinkedIn and email to a market they will live in for years cannot; one embarrassing message to a prospect who knows your investors costs more than ten messages never sent. That is why stage, not feature lists, should drive the choice, which is where evaluation starts.

How to evaluate an AI SDR

Four questions separate the tools faster than any demo call.

1. Start from your stage

Before product-market fit, your message changes weekly, because you are still learning what lands. An autonomous agent scales a pitch you have not validated yet; reviewing every send is not overhead at that stage, it is how you learn. If founder-led sales is working but eating your week, that is the moment for an agent that drafts while you keep judgment. Once a playbook is proven and the math is about coverage, autonomy buys real leverage.

2. Ask who approves the send

One direct question: does anything reach a prospect without a human reading it first? If the website will not answer it plainly, that is your answer. Approve-before-send, sometimes described as human-in-the-loop, costs you minutes a day and is the difference between an agent that works for you and one that speaks as you unsupervised.

3. Price the all-in cost, not the subscription

The subscription is the start. Add data and enrichment credits where they are metered, the extra mailboxes, domains, and warmup tools where the vendor sends from its own infrastructure, and your own time reviewing or cleaning up whatever the tool gets wrong. Published entry prices in the category run from $250 a month (Artisan, self-serve) to around $900 a month (AiSDR); the enterprise agents, 11x among them, are custom quoted and publish no pricing. driftwood publishes its range: $1,000 to $5,000 a month depending on send volume and channels, with details on the FAQ page. Whichever tool you pick, get the all-in number in writing before you compare.

4. Ask whose accounts it sends from

Two setups exist. Tools that send from mailboxes and domains stood up for the campaign protect your main domain, but the message arrives from an address the prospect has never seen and replies land in a tool inbox. Tools that send from your own accounts put your name and reputation behind every message, which is stronger, and is exactly why each of those messages deserves review before it goes. Be wary of the combination of high volume, your accounts, and no approval step: that is your reputation spent at machine speed.

Do AI SDRs actually work?

It depends which number you want. They reliably produce more sends per dollar. But the public studies collected on our benchmarks page show average cold email replies under 1% of sends at scale, so more generic sends mostly produce more ignored emails. Replies change when the message carries something specific the prospect can verify. Our own data: replies went from under 1% to over 14% in week one at Autosana (YC S25) when every message carried a working demo.

AI SDR vs human SDR: what is the difference?

Cost and judgment. Public salary data puts the median US SDR at $60,000 base and $85,000 on-target earnings (RepVue, March 2026), before benefits, tools, data, and management time. An AI SDR costs a fraction of that and covers more prospects, but it has no judgment of its own: left unsupervised, it produces generic outreach at scale. The setups that work keep a human in the loop, either a rep using the software or a founder approving an agent's sends.
